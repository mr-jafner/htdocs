The shorter version

I've spent roughly fifteen years in automotive engineering — first on gasoline direct injection fuel systems, now on turbomachinery and fuel cell compressors. Along the way I noticed something: the most interesting problems live at the seams.

Where mechanical hands off to electrical. Where methodology meets messy reality. Where one engineer's clear answer is another engineer's open question. That's where I do my best work, and where I most enjoy helping a team do theirs.

On the side, I build software — mostly graph-based reasoning tools and methodology-aware project management. It started as a way to think more clearly about my day job. It turned into a small body of work I'm genuinely proud of.

How I work

01 — Style

Coach over commander

I get more out of small teams by developing the people on them than by directing their work. Talent assessments confirm what I already suspected: people development genuinely energizes me, more than scope or scale.

02 — Conflict

Disagreement is a signal

When two competent engineers disagree, something useful is being surfaced. My instinct is to make space for it — not to smooth it over. The fastest path to a good decision usually runs through the awkward conversation, not around it.

03 — Translation

Build bridges, not towers

A mechanical engineer, an electrical engineer, and a firmware engineer can all look at the same failure and describe three different problems. A lot of what I do is translation — making sure each one's language reaches the others.

04 — Method

Build to think

I treat models, FMEAs, and small software tools as ways of working through a problem — not deliverables to file away. If a methodology can't shape my thinking on a real problem this week, I'm not sure what it's for.

A medical diagnostic reasoning tool that borrows DFMEA structure-tree conventions from automotive and points them at clinical reasoning. Scoped to the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis for v0.

A single graph model for SysML, DFMEA, and requirements analysis — methodologies that should have been talking to each other already. Aligned with SysML v2 vocabulary; deployed at jafner.com/synapse.

A timeline and Gantt-style project management tool with Git-like branching. Useful when plans need to fork — which, in cross-functional engineering, is most of the time.
